# FUZE Strategic Partner Model

## Executive Summary

FUZE uses strategic partnerships to place practical products in the hands of users who can benefit from them. The objective is not to collect partner logos or announce relationships without an operating plan. A useful partnership must connect a defined audience, problem, product workflow, delivery owner, and measurable result.

Partners can contribute distribution, technical integration, implementation capacity, specialist expertise, infrastructure, devices, communities, education programs, events, or local market access. FUZE can contribute AI SaaS products, shared platform capabilities, controlled workspaces, implementation patterns, product documentation, reporting, and co-designed use cases.

Every relationship moves through a structured process from discovery and fit assessment to due diligence, pilot activation, measurement, renewal, revision, or closure. Commercial duties, data access, support ownership, public communication, and evidence requirements are agreed before activation. Partner access is limited by role, workspace, purpose, and duration, with records retained according to the operating need.

Prospective partners and investors can use this model to evaluate how FUZE turns collaboration into accountable product expansion. It favors focused deployments with user value and credible evidence over unsupported announcements.

## 1. Why Strategic Partnerships Matter to FUZE

FUZE products can solve practical problems, but product capability alone does not create adoption. Users also need discovery, implementation, training, trusted local support, compatible technology, and workflows that fit the environments where they already operate.

Strategic partners can close those gaps. A point-of-sale provider may connect hardware and transaction workflows to ShopOS AI. An agency may configure the product for several restaurants. A university may use TrainLayer AI in a practical learning program. A community operator may introduce CommunityLayer AI inside an existing Telegram environment. In each case, the partner contributes something FUZE should not assume it can reproduce efficiently on its own.

Controlled deployments can reveal onboarding friction, integration requirements, support demand, pricing constraints, permission needs, and product improvements. That evidence can guide product and go-to-market decisions without treating one pilot as broad market proof.

The [FUZE Go-To-Market model](./03-FUZE_GO_TO_MARKET_PUBLIC.md) explains the wider distribution direction. This paper focuses specifically on how an individual partnership becomes an operating relationship with owners, controls, and review points.

## 2. Who This Model Is For

This model is relevant to organizations that can contribute practical delivery or reach, including:

- AI SaaS and technology providers seeking product or API integration
- shop groups, SME networks, agencies, and local implementers
- enterprises that require governed workspaces and defined deployment ownership
- distributors that can reach a specific user segment or geography
- community operators and Web3 ecosystems with established member workflows
- game communities and ecosystem teams exploring ZAGA-related activities
- universities, training providers, and workforce-development programs
- event organizers, venue operators, sponsors, and media partners
- infrastructure, device, data, security, research, and compliance providers
- investors reviewing FUZE partnership readiness and operating discipline

A recognized name, large audience, or access to capital is insufficient by itself. The organization must help deliver a defined user outcome and own its part of the work.

## 3. FUZE Partnership Principles

FUZE applies six principles when evaluating and operating strategic relationships.

### 3.1 Start With a User Outcome

Each partnership begins with a real user group and a problem that a FUZE product can address. The agreement should identify what changes for those users if the relationship works.

### 3.2 Define Ownership Before Activation

Product configuration, integration work, user onboarding, support, training, security, communications, and reporting need named owners. Shared responsibility is documented rather than assumed.

### 3.3 Use the Smallest Credible Scope

An initial pilot should be large enough to generate useful evidence but narrow enough to control operational and data risk. Expansion follows reviewed performance, not announcement momentum.

### 3.4 Limit Access to Purpose

Partner access follows the minimum necessary role, workspace, data set, system function, and time period. Credentials and permissions are reviewed when scope changes.

### 3.5 Measure Evidence, Not Visibility Alone

Public attention can support distribution, but it is not the primary measure of partner value. FUZE looks for completed integrations, activated organizations, sustained product usage, training completion, implementation quality, support performance, and other evidence appropriate to the use case.

### 3.6 Communicate With Accuracy

Public recognition, case studies, metrics, and partner statements require approval from the responsible parties. Announcements should describe the actual status of the relationship and avoid implying deployment, adoption, revenue, or endorsement beyond available evidence.

## 4. Strategic Partner Categories

FUZE uses categories to clarify a partner's main operating role. One organization may perform more than one role, but each responsibility should remain identifiable.

| Partner Category | Practical Role |
|---|---|
| Product and Technology Partners | Connect APIs, devices, models, data, infrastructure, or specialist tools to a defined FUZE workflow |
| Distribution and Market-Entry Partners | Introduce products to a specific audience, region, industry, or channel and support qualified onboarding |
| SME Networks and Implementers | Configure products for shops or small businesses, train operators, and provide local assistance |
| Enterprise Partners | Sponsor governed deployments with internal owners, workspace controls, integration review, and operating support |
| Community and Ecosystem Partners | Apply community, Web3, or game-related products within established member environments |
| Education and Event Partners | Deliver learning programs, event workflows, demonstrations, or participant services |
| Sponsored Visibility Partners | Use reviewed, clearly identified ToolGrid AI inventory with agreed placement and reporting terms |
| Research, Security, and Compliance Partners | Contribute testing, evidence, standards, privacy review, safety review, or specialist operating guidance |

The category is not a status label. It helps determine the qualification criteria, contract structure, implementation plan, permission profile, metrics, and exit process.

## 5. Partnership Value Exchange

A sustainable partnership requires value on both sides and a clear benefit for the user.

FUZE may provide:

- access to relevant products and supported platform functions
- integration specifications and implementation guidance
- controlled accounts, roles, and workspaces
- product documentation and training materials
- co-designed workflows for a defined audience
- technical coordination and product support
- usage, implementation, or campaign reporting where available
- approved public recognition supported by accurate evidence

Partners may provide:

- access to users, organizations, communities, or distribution channels
- technical systems, devices, data connections, or infrastructure
- implementation staff and local operational support
- domain knowledge and workflow requirements
- user training, change management, and first-line assistance
- event, education, content, or community access
- structured product feedback and issue evidence
- compliance, security, research, or review capacity

The value exchange is recorded in practical terms. For example, “market access” should identify the target segment, partner activity, FUZE product, onboarding path, responsible owner, and evidence that will be reviewed. Broad claims of reach are insufficient without a delivery method.

Dependencies are reviewed before a pilot. Distribution depends on product readiness, while credible attribution depends on designed tracking, consent, and reporting.

## 6. Product and Platform Integration Models

Partnerships should use the product that fits the job rather than repeat the full FUZE ecosystem in every proposal. The [FUZE AI SaaS Product Index](../AI-SAAS-PRODUCT-PAPERS/01-FUZE_AI_SAAS_PRODUCT_INDEX_PUBLIC.md) provides the wider product map, while [FUZE Core Platform Rails](../CORE-PLATFORM-PAPERS/04-FUZE_CORE_PLATFORM_RAILS_PUBLIC.md) explains shared infrastructure available where needed.

| Example | Partner Role and Product | User Value | Integration and Owner | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| POS provider | Connect supported hardware or transaction data with ShopOS AI | Shop operators use a more coordinated operating workflow | Provider owns device compatibility; FUZE owns product behavior; both validate the interface | Successful test cases, activated locations, error records, and support response |
| Restaurant agency | Configure ShopOS AI for a defined restaurant group | Operators receive onboarding, setup, and local assistance | Agency owns configuration and first-line support; FUZE owns product documentation and escalation | Time to activation, staff training, usage, retention, and support quality |
| University | Use TrainLayer AI in a practical AI education program | Learners create and review guided materials and exercises | University owns curriculum and learner administration; FUZE supports product setup | Course completion, product activity, instructor feedback, and reviewed outputs |
| Telegram community | Apply CommunityLayer AI to moderation, support, or reporting workflows | Community operators gain structured assistance and clearer records | Community owner sets policy; FUZE product supports approved workflows | Onboarding completion, report delivery, response handling, and incident status |
| Event organizer | Use AIE for event intelligence and SpeakShop AI for approved announcements | Organizers coordinate information and participant communication | Organizer owns event content and approvals; FUZE supports product configuration | Participant use, report delivery, content approval, and post-event review |
| Tool provider | Join ToolGrid AI through a standard listing or sponsored placement | Users discover a relevant utility with sponsorship disclosed where applicable | Provider supplies accurate content; FUZE controls review, labeling, scope, and reporting | Approved visibility or engagement measures for the agreed period |
| Game ecosystem | Work with ZAGA on community activities, content, or supported integrations | Players or community members receive a defined game-related experience | Partner owns community delivery; FUZE owns relevant product and integration controls | Participation, completed activities, technical results, and community feedback |

These patterns are scoped against current product readiness, user needs, technical feasibility, and permission requirements.

Shared platform functions may support identity, workspaces, billing, product usage, AI routing, audit records, or reporting. A partnership should consume those functions through approved interfaces rather than create an independent credit, identity, or reporting system.

## 7. Strategic Partner Lifecycle

FUZE uses a twelve-stage lifecycle so that progress, decisions, and closure remain visible.

| Stage | Required Outcome |
|---:|---|
| 1. Discovery | Record the partner, audience, problem, proposed contribution, and initial product hypothesis |
| 2. Fit assessment | Test user relevance, product alignment, delivery capacity, support ability, reputation, and strategic value |
| 3. Use-case definition | Define the workflow, target users, expected benefit, scope, exclusions, owners, dependencies, and evidence |
| 4. Due diligence | Review relevant technical, security, data, legal, jurisdiction, reputation, conflict, and misleading-marketing concerns |
| 5. Commercial and responsibility design | Set fees, costs, payment duties, service ownership, support levels, communication approval, and escalation paths |
| 6. Data and permission review | Identify required data, ownership, consent, roles, workspace boundaries, retention, and access-removal rules |
| 7. Technical validation | Test interfaces, credentials, devices, data mappings, performance, failure handling, and support readiness |
| 8. Pilot or controlled activation | Launch the approved scope with named operators, monitoring, user support, and rollback or pause procedures |
| 9. Measurement | Compare agreed metrics with evidence, document incidents and feedback, and assess user value |
| 10. Expansion or renewal | Continue, broaden, or renew only after reviewing delivery, economics, capacity, permissions, and product readiness |
| 11. Revision, suspension, or offboarding | Correct scope, pause access, or end the relationship when requirements are unmet or conditions change |
| 12. Record retention and final reporting | Close access, reconcile responsibilities, retain required evidence, and issue the appropriate internal or public-safe summary |

Qualification is proportional to the relationship. A limited event workflow may need less review than an enterprise data integration, but both require ownership, permission, evidence, and closure planning.

Fit assessment covers user relevance, delivery capacity, technical readiness, data practices, support, measurable contribution, conflicts, jurisdiction, reputation, and public messaging risk.

Decision status should remain clear. A relationship may be exploratory, under review, in technical validation, piloting, active, paused, renewing, or closed. Public communication should not describe an exploratory discussion as an active deployment.

## 8. Commercial and Operating Responsibilities

The commercial model should match the work performed. Depending on the use case, an arrangement may include subscriptions, implementation or service fees, usage-based charges, referral or distribution terms, sponsored visibility packages, enterprise agreements, co-funded pilots, or contributor and vendor compensation.

For eligible product actions, Platform Credits may be assigned to the responsible account or workspace under the applicable usage rules. Stablecoin settlement can be used in an approved payment or compensation flow when operationally appropriate. FUZE token should appear in a partner program only when an ecosystem-level utility is genuinely part of that program. Token-related wallet records may support public-safe evidence without exposing the identity behind a wallet.

Commercial documentation should identify:

- who contracts, invoices, collects, pays, refunds, or reconciles
- which party bears implementation, infrastructure, device, travel, support, or campaign costs
- how referrals, service delivery, sponsored activity, or usage are attributed
- which taxes, fees, currencies, and settlement procedures require separate review
- what happens to prepaid usage, open invoices, partner assets, and user support at closure

Operating responsibilities require similar precision.

| Area | FUZE Responsibility | Partner Responsibility | Joint Responsibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product | Maintain the supported product scope and document known operating requirements | Use the product within the approved purpose and provide accurate requirements | Prioritize validated issues and changes |
| Implementation | Supply relevant configuration and integration guidance | Provide assigned staff, local setup, and required systems | Approve the activation plan and acceptance tests |
| Users | Define supported onboarding and escalation paths | Recruit or assign eligible users and deliver first-line guidance where agreed | Review user feedback and unresolved support demand |
| Communications | Approve FUZE product and status statements | Approve partner names, marks, and attributed statements | Confirm public metrics and case-study language |
| Incidents | Investigate FUZE product or platform issues | Report partner-system, user, or local operating issues promptly | Coordinate containment, correction, notice, and follow-up |

## 9. Data, Privacy, Permissions, and Security

Partner access is designed around the least access required to deliver the approved use case. Permissions are assigned by role and workspace, separated from unrelated customers or partners, and reviewed when staff, scope, or systems change.

Before activation, the parties identify:

- data ownership and the authorized source for each record
- confidential information and permitted uses
- customer, learner, employee, event, or community records involved
- API keys, tokens, device credentials, and integration secrets
- storage, transfer, retention, deletion, and export expectations
- audit logs and evidence needed for support or review
- incident contacts, response duties, and correction procedures
- subcontractor or vendor access where applicable
- permission removal and credential rotation at offboarding

Public reporting may identify a partner, use case, status, aggregated activity, approved metric, or reviewed outcome. It should not publish private negotiations, customer-level records, API secrets, non-public commercial terms, personal identities, sensitive security procedures, legal advice, or accounting workpapers.

Wallet addresses may be used in token-related records where useful, but a public wallet reference should not be presented as personal identity. Additional privacy and permission direction is available in the [FUZE Data Privacy and Permission Model](./08-FUZE_DATA_PRIVACY_AND_PERMISSION_MODEL_PUBLIC.md).

An incident can lead to restricted access, credential rotation, data correction, a narrower scope, temporary suspension, or offboarding. The response should preserve relevant logs and assign follow-up actions rather than relying only on informal communication.

## 10. Reporting and Partnership Performance

Metrics are selected per partnership. A shop deployment, university program, sponsored placement, enterprise integration, and community pilot should not share one generic success score.

Possible measures include:

- activated organizations, locations, workspaces, or approved user groups
- completed integrations and validated technical tests
- onboarded users and sustained product activity
- pilot completion and time to implementation
- training attendance and completion
- support response, resolution quality, and unresolved issue volume
- retention or renewal within the reviewed scope
- event participation and delivery of agreed reports
- partner-generated product feedback accepted for review
- security, privacy, incident, and compliance status
- delivery against milestones, service duties, and reporting dates

The measurement plan identifies the source, owner, calculation method, review cadence, and public/private classification for each metric. When evidence is incomplete, the report should say so rather than converting estimates into confirmed results.

Internal partner reports can include commercial performance, support records, detailed user activity, technical findings, incidents, and corrective actions. Public-safe reporting is narrower and may use approved aggregate metrics, deployment status, case-study evidence, or a concise outcome summary. The broader reporting approach is described in [FUZE Public Metrics and Transparency](./09-FUZE_PUBLIC_METRICS_AND_TRANSPARENCY_PUBLIC.md).

Performance review should produce a decision: continue, revise, expand, renew, pause, or close the relationship.

## 11. Renewal, Suspension, and Offboarding

Renewal is a new operating decision, not an automatic extension. FUZE and the partner review user value, delivery quality, product readiness, commercial performance, support capacity, permissions, incidents, and any proposed change in scope.

Revision may be appropriate when the use case remains valid but ownership, integration, audience, pricing, support, or measurement needs correction. A revised plan records the new scope and acceptance conditions before additional access or public claims are approved.

Suspension may be used when there is a security or privacy concern, material delivery failure, misuse of product access, misleading public communication, unresolved commercial issue, legal or jurisdiction concern, reputational risk, or loss of the required operating owner. During suspension, affected permissions, integrations, campaigns, or public references can be limited while the issue is reviewed.

Offboarding closes the operating relationship. The process should:

1. confirm the effective date and user-support plan
2. stop new activity and remove partner permissions
3. revoke or rotate credentials and disconnect integrations
4. return, export, retain, or delete records according to the approved requirements
5. reconcile invoices, usage, partner assets, and open responsibilities
6. update public status or approved partner references where necessary
7. preserve required audit, incident, decision, and final-report records

Closure does not erase accurate historical evidence, but retained records remain subject to their access and privacy classification. A final review should capture lessons for product, implementation, qualification, and future partner design.

## 12. Public Boundary

FUZE partnerships activate through defined product, technical, commercial, permission, and reporting requirements. Results depend on user fit, execution, partner delivery, and operating conditions. Detailed token, market, financial, and investment boundaries are covered in the [FUZE Risk and Disclosure Appendix](../WHITEPAPER-PAPERS/05-FUZE_RISK_AND_DISCLOSURE_APPENDIX_PUBLIC.md).

## 13. Key Takeaways

- A FUZE strategic partnership begins with a specific user outcome and a partner contribution that can be operated and measured.
- Partner categories clarify roles; they do not substitute for qualification or delivery evidence.
- Product, support, commercial, communication, data, and incident responsibilities are assigned before activation.
- Controlled pilots create evidence for renewal or expansion without assuming broad adoption.
- Access follows purpose, role, workspace, and duration, with explicit removal when the relationship ends.
- Each partnership uses its own metrics and separates internal records from public-safe reporting.
- Revision, suspension, and offboarding are normal lifecycle decisions when scope, performance, or operating conditions change.
- The strongest partner relationships leave FUZE with better product evidence, clearer workflows, and practical value for users.
