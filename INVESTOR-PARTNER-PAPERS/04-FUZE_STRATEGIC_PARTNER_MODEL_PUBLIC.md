# FUZE Strategic Partner Model

## Executive Summary

FUZE uses strategic partnerships to place practical products into environments where users can receive measurable value. The objective is not to collect partner logos, imply relationships from preliminary discussions, or announce collaborations before an operating plan exists. A useful partnership connects a defined audience, user problem, FUZE product workflow, partner contribution, accountable owners, controlled access, commercial treatment, and reviewable outcome.

Partners may contribute distribution, technical integration, implementation capacity, domain expertise, infrastructure, devices, communities, education programs, events, or local market access. FUZE may contribute AI SaaS products, shared platform capabilities, controlled workspaces, implementation guidance, product documentation, reporting, and co-designed use cases.

Every relationship moves through a controlled lifecycle from discovery and fit assessment to due diligence, use-case definition, technical validation, pilot activation, measurement, renewal, expansion, revision, suspension, or closure. Public status must match the actual stage. A discussion is not a partnership, a signed memorandum is not a deployment, a pilot is not broad adoption, and a public announcement is not evidence of revenue or operating success.

Commercial duties, data access, support ownership, incident responsibilities, communication approval, and evidence requirements should be agreed before activation. Partner access remains limited by role, workspace, purpose, environment, and duration. Records are retained according to legal, commercial, privacy, security, support, and reporting needs.

This paper gives prospective partners and investors a public framework for evaluating how FUZE turns collaboration into controlled product expansion. It favors focused deployments, user value, operating accountability, and credible evidence over unsupported reach or visibility claims.

## 1. Purpose and Primary Readers

This paper is written for prospective strategic partners, investors, product owners, commercial operators, implementation teams, technical reviewers, and governance reviewers.

It explains:

1. which partner types are relevant to FUZE;
2. how a partner opportunity is qualified;
3. how product, commercial, data, technical, support, and communication responsibilities are assigned;
4. which evidence supports pilot, active, renewal, expansion, or closure status;
5. how FUZE limits access and protects confidential information;
6. how partner performance is measured;
7. how relationships are revised, suspended, or offboarded;
8. which public claims are permitted at each stage.

The [FUZE Go-To-Market](03-FUZE_GO_TO_MARKET_PUBLIC.md) paper governs the wider distribution model. This paper governs the operating structure of an individual strategic relationship.

## 2. Current Public Position

FUZE's public documents describe partner categories, example use cases, qualification requirements, operating responsibilities, reporting methods, and lifecycle controls.

They do not by themselves prove:

- that a named organization is a FUZE partner;
- that a discussion, introduction, or memorandum has become an operating relationship;
- that an integration has been implemented or activated;
- that a pilot has begun or completed;
- that users, customers, locations, or communities have adopted a product;
- that a partner has generated revenue, distribution, retention, or strategic value;
- that a public metric, case study, endorsement, or logo use has been approved;
- that a partner has rights involving FUZE token, Platform Credits, treasury assets, market access, or investor outcomes.

A stronger partnership claim requires evidence appropriate to the exact stage, such as a signed agreement, approved use case, technical validation, activated deployment, user records, support records, commercial reconciliation, or approved public statement.

## 3. Why Strategic Partnerships Matter

Product capability alone does not create adoption. Users may also need:

- trusted discovery and local reach;
- implementation and configuration;
- compatible devices, systems, or data;
- training and change management;
- first-line support;
- specialist security, legal, privacy, or compliance review;
- event, community, or education access;
- operating knowledge specific to an industry or geography.

Strategic partners can close these gaps where their contribution is more credible or efficient than FUZE attempting to reproduce it internally.

Controlled partner deployments can also reveal:

- onboarding friction;
- integration requirements;
- support demand;
- product defects;
- pricing constraints;
- localization needs;
- permission and data risks;
- channel quality;
- operating cost;
- product improvements.

One successful pilot should not be treated as proof of broad market fit. Partner evidence should remain scoped to the product, audience, location, period, and responsibilities actually reviewed.

## 4. Partnership Principles

### 4.1 Begin with a user outcome

Every relationship should identify the user group, problem, product workflow, and observable result.

### 4.2 Use the smallest credible scope

The initial scope should be large enough to create useful evidence but narrow enough to control technical, commercial, privacy, support, and reputational risk.

### 4.3 Assign ownership before activation

Product, implementation, onboarding, support, data, security, communications, reporting, and incident duties need named owners.

### 4.4 Limit access to purpose

Partners receive only the workspace, role, data, tools, credentials, and duration required for the approved use case.

### 4.5 Separate status from visibility

A logo, announcement, meeting, memorandum, or public audience does not prove implementation, adoption, revenue, or endorsement.

### 4.6 Measure evidence rather than activity volume

Partner value is assessed through completed work, activated users, reliable delivery, product usage, support quality, commercial evidence, and reviewed outcomes.

### 4.7 Preserve product and system boundaries

A partner should use approved FUZE products and shared rails rather than create independent identity, Platform Credit, wallet, reporting, or token systems that conflict with the FUZE operating model.

### 4.8 Keep public communication accurate

Names, marks, metrics, quotes, case studies, commercial claims, and relationship status require appropriate approval.

## 5. Partner Categories

One organization may perform more than one role, but each role should remain identifiable.

| Partner category | Primary contribution |
|---|---|
| Product and technology partner | APIs, devices, models, data, infrastructure, or specialist tools |
| Distribution and market-entry partner | Access to a defined audience, region, industry, or channel |
| SME network or implementer | Local configuration, onboarding, training, and first-line support |
| Enterprise partner | Governed deployment, internal ownership, integration, and operational adoption |
| Community or ecosystem partner | Established member workflows, moderation, events, or community delivery |
| Game partner | Game community access, content, events, integrations, or supported operations |
| Education or training partner | Curriculum, learners, facilitators, and structured learning delivery |
| Event, venue, or media partner | Event access, participant workflows, content, sponsorship, or reporting |
| Sponsored visibility partner | Clearly identified ToolGrid AI listing or campaign inventory |
| Research, security, or compliance partner | Testing, standards, privacy, security, assurance, or specialist review |
| Infrastructure or service provider | Hosting, communications, payment, data, device, or operational services |
| Strategic investor-partner | Capital plus a defined operating, distribution, technical, or strategic contribution |

A category is not a relationship status. It helps determine qualification, contract structure, permissions, measures, and offboarding requirements.

## 6. Partner Qualification

A recognized brand, large audience, capital access, or technical capability is insufficient by itself.

Qualification should assess:

- user relevance and product fit;
- clarity of the proposed contribution;
- delivery capacity and named owners;
- technical compatibility;
- data, privacy, and security practices;
- support and escalation capability;
- commercial and financial reliability;
- jurisdiction and operating location;
- legal and compliance dependencies;
- conflicts of interest;
- reputation and misleading-marketing risk;
- willingness to provide evidence and accept operating controls;
- offboarding feasibility.

A partner opportunity may be rejected or deferred when:

- no defined user outcome exists;
- the product is not ready for the required scope;
- the partner expects unsupported exclusivity, token, market, or financial claims;
- the required access is disproportionate;
- ownership and support duties remain unclear;
- public promotion is prioritized ahead of implementation;
- data, security, legal, or reputational risks cannot be controlled;
- the relationship lacks a credible commercial or learning objective.

## 7. Partnership Value Exchange

A sustainable partnership should create value for the user and define the contribution of each party.

FUZE may provide:

- access to relevant products and approved shared platform capabilities;
- integration specifications and implementation guidance;
- controlled accounts, roles, and workspaces;
- product documentation and training materials;
- technical coordination and escalation;
- co-designed workflows for a defined audience;
- usage, implementation, or campaign reporting where available;
- approved public recognition supported by evidence.

Partners may provide:

- access to users, organizations, communities, or distribution channels;
- technical systems, devices, infrastructure, models, or data connections;
- implementation staff and local support;
- domain knowledge and workflow requirements;
- training, change management, and first-line assistance;
- event, education, content, or community access;
- structured product feedback and issue evidence;
- specialist security, privacy, legal, research, or compliance capacity;
- capital tied to a defined operating purpose where applicable.

Broad terms such as “market access,” “strategic support,” or “ecosystem growth” should be translated into a target segment, partner action, FUZE product, owner, timeline, and evidence source.

## 8. Example Product and Partner Models

| Example | Partner role and product | User value | Ownership | Evidence |

| Restaurant or SME agency | Configure ShopOS AI for a defined operator group | Local onboarding and operating support | Agency owns setup and first-line support; FUZE owns product and escalation | Activation time, training, usage, retention, support quality |
| University or training provider | Use TrainLayer AI in a structured program | Guided content creation and review | Partner owns curriculum and learner administration; FUZE supports product setup | Completion, activity, instructor review, learner feedback |

| Event organizer | Use AIE and SpeakShop AI for event planning and communication | More organized event delivery and reporting | Organizer owns event information and approvals; FUZE supports configuration | Workflow completion, approved content, participant evidence, recap |
| Tool provider | Use ToolGrid AI listing or sponsored visibility | Reviewed utility discovery | Provider supplies accurate information; FUZE controls review and labeling | Approved placement, period, destination status, report |
| Game ecosystem | Work with ZAGA on activities, content, or supported integrations | Defined game or community experience | Partner owns community delivery; FUZE owns product and technical controls | Participation, completed activities, technical results, feedback |

## 9. Partnership Lifecycle

FUZE uses a staged lifecycle so that decisions and status remain visible.

| Stage | Required outcome |
|---:|---|
| 1. Introduction | Record the organization, contact, source, and proposed relationship |
| 2. Discovery | Identify audience, user problem, partner contribution, and product hypothesis |
| 3. Fit assessment | Review relevance, capacity, support, reputation, strategic value, and conflicts |
| 4. Use-case definition | Define workflow, users, scope, exclusions, owners, dependencies, and evidence |
| 5. Due diligence | Review technical, commercial, security, privacy, legal, jurisdiction, and communication risks |
| 6. Commercial and responsibility design | Define contracts, fees, costs, support, ownership, reporting, and escalation |
| 7. Data and permission review | Define data authority, roles, workspaces, access, retention, deletion, and offboarding |
| 8. Technical validation | Test interfaces, credentials, devices, data mappings, performance, and failure handling |
| 9. Pilot approval | Confirm readiness, operators, support, monitoring, pause, and rollback arrangements |
| 10. Controlled pilot | Operate the approved scope and collect product, support, and commercial evidence |
| 11. Review | Assess user value, delivery, economics, incidents, feedback, and open obligations |
| 12. Renew, expand, revise, pause, or close | Make and record the next operating decision |
| 13. Final reconciliation and reporting | Close access, obligations, records, and approved public status |

Review depth should be proportional to consequence. A limited event workflow may require less review than an enterprise integration or payment provider, but both require ownership, access control, evidence, and closure planning.

## 10. Partnership Status Vocabulary

Use the narrowest accurate status.

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Introduced | Initial contact or referral exists |
| In discussion | Preliminary communication is underway |
| Exploratory | A possible use case is being considered without operating commitment |
| Under review | Fit, diligence, scope, or approval remains incomplete |
| Use case defined | Product, users, owners, scope, and expected evidence are documented |
| Agreement signed | An agreement exists for the stated scope, subject to its conditions |
| In technical validation | Integration or operating requirements are being tested |
| Pilot approved | The controlled pilot has completed required approval gates |
| Piloting | The defined pilot is operating |
| Active | The approved operating relationship is delivering its current scope |
| Expanding | A reviewed extension is being implemented |
| Renewing | Continuation is under review or agreement renewal is in process |
| Paused | Identified activity or access is temporarily restricted |
| Closed | The operating relationship has ended and closure duties are being or have been completed |

A signed memorandum, letter of intent, announcement, or technical conversation should not be described as an active partnership unless operating evidence supports that status.

## 11. Commercial Model

The commercial structure should match the work performed. Possible arrangements include:

- subscriptions or workspace plans;
- implementation or service fees;
- usage-based charges;
- referral or distribution fees;
- sponsored visibility packages;
- enterprise agreements;
- co-funded pilots;
- training or event packages;
- vendor or contributor compensation;
- strategic investment with separate operating terms.

Commercial records should identify:

- contracting parties;
- offer and scope;
- who invoices, collects, pays, refunds, and reconciles;
- payment route, currency, asset, and fees where applicable;
- implementation, infrastructure, device, travel, support, and campaign costs;
- attribution for referrals, service delivery, campaigns, or usage;
- taxes and jurisdictional dependencies;
- prepaid service, Platform Credit, and unused-balance treatment;
- renewal, termination, suspension, and closure obligations.

Platform Credits may support eligible product actions in the appropriate account or workspace. They remain separate from FUZE token.

Stablecoins may support an approved payment or compensation workflow. A stablecoin transfer does not by itself prove partner status, product delivery, revenue, or settlement completion.

FUZE token should appear in a partner program only when a defined and approved ecosystem utility requires it. Partnership status does not create token rights, market access, liquidity, allocation, or investor entitlement.

## 12. Responsibility Matrix

| Area | FUZE responsibility | Partner responsibility | Joint responsibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product | Maintain the supported product scope and known limitations | Use the product within approved purpose and provide accurate requirements | Prioritize validated issues and changes |
| Implementation | Provide applicable specifications and configuration guidance | Assign staff, systems, local setup, and implementation duties | Approve plan and acceptance criteria |
| Users | Define supported onboarding and escalation routes | Recruit or assign qualified users and provide agreed first-line guidance | Review feedback and unresolved demand |
| Data | Operate FUZE-side controls and approved product handling | Confirm data authority, accuracy, consent, and permitted use | Approve data flow and correction route |
| Security | Protect FUZE systems and investigate FUZE-side issues | Protect partner systems, credentials, devices, and users | Coordinate incidents and remediation |
| Commercial | Operate FUZE billing, credits, or settlement duties in scope | Perform partner invoicing, collection, referral, or payment duties in scope | Reconcile shared commercial events |
| Support | Provide product support and escalation within scope | Provide local or first-line support where agreed | Review service quality and open issues |
| Communications | Approve FUZE product, status, metric, and mark use | Approve partner name, mark, quote, and attributed statement | Approve announcements and case studies |
| Reporting | Define FUZE source events and public-safe treatment | Provide accurate partner-side records | Review results, limitations, and corrections |
| Closure | Remove FUZE-side access and complete FUZE obligations | Remove partner-side access and complete partner obligations | Reconcile users, data, assets, support, and public status |

Shared responsibility should not mean unassigned responsibility. A named owner is still required for each operational decision.

## 13. Technical Integration

A technical partnership should define:

- systems, environments, networks, devices, APIs, or providers involved;
- interface ownership and versioning;
- authentication and authorization;
- test data and production data boundaries;
- rate, capacity, performance, and availability assumptions;
- errors, retries, timeouts, and failure handling;
- monitoring and support;
- change approval and compatibility;
- deployment and activation status;
- rollback, pause, and recovery;
- credential rotation and offboarding.

Technical validation should produce reviewable evidence such as test cases, interface results, defects, acceptance decisions, and open limitations.

Technical integration does not establish user adoption, commercial success, endorsement, or a broader partnership than the approved scope.

## 14. Data, Privacy, Permissions, and Security

Partner access follows least privilege and purpose limitation.

Before activation, the parties should identify:

- data owner and authoritative source;
- permitted purpose;
- personal, confidential, restricted, and public-safe fields;
- users, roles, and workspace boundaries;
- API keys, tokens, credentials, devices, and secrets;
- storage, transfer, retention, deletion, correction, and export requirements;
- logs and evidence required for support or review;
- incident contacts and notification duties;
- subcontractor or provider access;
- permission removal and credential rotation at closure.

A partner should not receive access to unrelated customers, workspaces, products, financial records, investor records, private wallets, credentials, or source systems.

Public wallet or transaction records may be used where relevant, but a wallet address should not be presented as the identity of a person without authorized evidence and disclosure purpose.

The [FUZE Data Privacy and Permission Model](08-FUZE_DATA_PRIVACY_AND_PERMISSION_MODEL_PUBLIC.md) governs the wider treatment.

## 15. Communications and Public Claims

Public communication may identify an approved partner, use case, relationship stage, product, pilot scope, reviewed metric, or outcome where both parties authorize publication.

It should not expose:

- private negotiations;
- unannounced relationships;
- customer-level or user-level records;
- confidential commercial terms;
- personal identity;
- credentials, secrets, or exploitable technical detail;
- privileged legal or professional advice;
- accounting workpapers;
- unsupported projections or endorsements.

Common corrections include:

| Misleading wording | Preferred treatment |
|---|---|
| “FUZE partnered with X” after an introduction | State that an introduction or exploratory discussion occurred, if publication is approved |
| “Integration complete” after initial testing | State the tested component, environment, result, and remaining gates |
| “Deployed across the partner network” after one pilot | State the exact pilot scope and status |
| “Partner brings millions of users” | State the audience or channel and the approved onboarding method without implying conversion |
| “Revenue partnership” before paid delivery | State the commercial model or agreement stage |
| “Officially endorsed” | Use only with explicit approved endorsement evidence |
| “Exclusive partner” | Use only when an authorized agreement defines exclusivity, scope, geography, and period |
| “Strategic investor and partner” | Distinguish investment status from the separate operating contribution |

## 16. Pilot Design

A controlled pilot should define:

- user group and eligibility;
- product and exact workflow;
- locations, accounts, workspaces, or communities;
- start and end dates;
- partner and FUZE owners;
- implementation and training plan;
- access and data boundaries;
- support and escalation;
- commercial treatment;
- activation and completion events;
- measures and evidence sources;
- success, revision, pause, and stop criteria;
- incident, rollback, and offboarding procedures;
- approved communication status.

A pilot should answer a specific product, operating, commercial, or distribution question. It should not exist only to support an announcement.

## 17. Measurement and Performance Review

Metrics should match the partnership's purpose.

Possible measures include:

- activated organizations, locations, workspaces, or approved user groups;
- completed integrations and technical validation;
- onboarding and training completion;
- time to first product value;
- sustained product activity;
- delivery and milestone completion;
- support response and resolution quality;
- unresolved issue volume;
- retention or renewal;
- event or program completion;
- partner-attributed qualified opportunities;
- paid delivery and commercial reconciliation;
- privacy, security, incident, and compliance status;
- accepted product feedback and resulting changes.

Each measure should identify:

- definition;
- source;
- owner;
- cohort or population;
- period;
- exclusions;
- correction method;
- public or restricted classification.

A performance review should produce one decision: continue, revise, expand, renew, pause, or close.

Public reporting should remain narrower than internal reporting and may use approved aggregate results, limitations, and correction notes.

## 18. Renewal and Expansion

Renewal is a new operating decision rather than an automatic continuation.

Review should cover:

- user value;
- product quality and readiness;
- partner delivery;
- support capacity;
- commercial performance;
- technical reliability;
- data and permissions;
- incidents and corrective actions;
- open obligations;
- proposed scope changes;
- public communication status.

Expansion should follow evidence from the initial scope. A larger geography, audience, product, integration, or commercial commitment should receive new approval and updated operating records.

## 19. Suspension

Suspension may be appropriate when there is:

- a security or privacy concern;
- material delivery failure;
- misuse of product or data access;
- misleading public communication;
- unresolved commercial or payment issue;
- legal or jurisdiction concern;
- reputational risk;
- unsupported token, market, or financial claims;
- loss of the required owner or support capability;
- breach of contract or operating requirements.

During suspension, affected access, integrations, campaigns, commercial activity, or public references may be restricted while the issue is reviewed.

Suspension should record scope, authority, reason, effective time, user treatment, evidence, corrective actions, and next-review criteria.

## 20. Offboarding and Closure

Offboarding should:

1. confirm the effective date and closure authority;
2. define the customer and user support plan;
3. stop new activity;
4. remove partner and FUZE permissions no longer required;
5. revoke or rotate credentials;
6. disconnect integrations and devices where applicable;
7. return, export, retain, or delete records according to approved requirements;
8. reconcile invoices, Platform Credits, usage, assets, support, and open obligations;
9. update public status and approved partner references;
10. preserve required decision, incident, audit, and final-report records;
11. capture lessons for product, qualification, implementation, and future partnerships.

Closure does not erase accurate historical evidence, but retained records remain subject to their privacy, confidentiality, and access classifications.

## 21. Investor Review Questions

Investors reviewing partner strategy should ask:

- Which relationships have progressed beyond discussion or announcement?
- Which partner deployments have completed technical validation?
- Which pilots have named users, owners, measures, and closure criteria?
- Which partners provide distribution, implementation, technology, or operating value that FUZE cannot efficiently reproduce?
- Which relationships have generated product usage, paid delivery, retention, or renewal evidence?
- How concentrated is FUZE on any one partner, provider, channel, geography, or system?
- Which relationships require sensitive data, privileged access, or material integration?
- How are partner costs, revenue, referrals, Platform Credits, and payments reconciled?
- How are incidents, misleading claims, access changes, and offboarding handled?
- Which public partner claims are supported by current evidence and approval?

## 22. Public Boundary

This paper defines FUZE's strategic-partner operating model.

It does not state that:

- a named organization is currently a FUZE partner;
- a discussion, memorandum, agreement, or announcement is an active deployment;
- any partner has delivered users, revenue, adoption, retention, investment, or market access;
- a partner relationship creates token rights, liquidity, listing, allocation, or investor return;
- a pilot will expand or renew;
- any partnership will achieve a commercial, technical, strategic, or public-market outcome.

Relationship status, scope, metrics, commercial terms, data access, public references, and results depend on current approved agreements and evidence.

## Key Takeaways

- A FUZE strategic partnership begins with a defined user outcome and a partner contribution that can be operated and measured.
- A discussion, memorandum, agreement, pilot, active deployment, expansion, and renewal are different stages.
- Partner categories clarify roles but do not substitute for qualification or evidence.
- Product, commercial, implementation, support, data, security, incident, reporting, and communication responsibilities are assigned before activation.
- Access follows purpose, role, workspace, environment, and duration.
- Controlled pilots create evidence without implying broad adoption.
- Platform Credits remain separate from FUZE token, and stablecoin transfers remain operational payment or settlement records.
- Revision, suspension, and offboarding are normal lifecycle decisions.
- The strongest partnerships leave FUZE with better product evidence, stronger delivery capacity, and practical value for users.
